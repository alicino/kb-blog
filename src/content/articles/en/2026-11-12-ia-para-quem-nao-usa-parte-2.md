People who use AI every now and then gain almost nothing. The gain comes from repetition, and repetition requires process, not inspiration.

This is the second of five posts. In the [first one](/en/articles/2026-11-11-ia-para-quem-nao-usa-parte-1/), the idea was to recognize a task worth doing. Here the idea is different: turn that task into something that happens every week without you having to remember it exists.

## The difference between using AI and having a process with AI

**Using AI:** you remember it exists, sometimes. The result is uneven and you can't tell whether it helped.

**Having a process:** there's a defined moment in your week when that task goes through AI, always the same way. The result is predictable.

The difference isn't technical. It's habit.

## How to choose your first process

The criterion is simple: **the task you repeat every week and hate doing.**

Don't pick the most important task. Pick the most boring, most repetitive one. The reason is practical: that's where the gain is most obvious, and where you won't give up because you think writing it yourself comes out better.

## The full case: meetings

Meetings are the best first process, because they have three moments and all three are textual.

**Before.** Generate the agenda from the goal: "I'm having a 30-minute meeting with the support team to decide whether we migrate customer service to another tool. Give me a 4-item agenda with a suggested time for each."

**During.** The automatic transcript, which most meeting tools already produce, becomes raw material.

**After.** This is the step almost nobody does: "Here's the transcript. List the decisions made, the actions with owner and deadline, and the questions that went unanswered."

That third step is what changes the game for Rafael. He stops leaving meetings with the feeling that he decided something without knowing exactly what.

## The same process, adapted to each case

**Camila, admin.** Her process is the minutes. Every meeting she attends becomes minutes in 10 minutes, in the same format, filed in the same place. She stops being "the one who takes minutes" and becomes "the one who has the history of decisions," which is a much harder position to replace.

**Rafael, project manager.** His process is the weekly status report. He keeps the week's notes and, on Friday, generates the executive summary. What used to take two hours now takes twenty minutes.

**Sandra, salesperson.** Her process is the post-visit follow-up. Every visit produces a summary email with what was agreed, the same day. Salespeople who follow up the same day close more, and she already knew that, she just didn't have the time.

Notice it's the same mechanism in all three cases. What changes is the task, not the method.

## Where AI shouldn't go

This list will come back in the last post of the series, but it needs to exist here:

1. Customer data with name, ID number, phone or address
2. Contract information, proposals with pricing, commercial terms
3. Anything your company considers confidential
4. Decisions with direct legal or financial consequence

**The rule of thumb:** if you wouldn't paste it into a WhatsApp group with people from work, don't paste it into an AI chat. It's simple and covers most cases.

## How to tell if it's working

Don't count how many requests you made. Count **time given back**.

The honest question at the end of the month: "what do I do now that I didn't do before, because time freed up?"

If the answer is "nothing," you automated and gained nothing. You just filled the time with more work. That's a bad outcome, and it's more common than it sounds.

## Closing

One repeated process is worth more than ten tricks tried once.

And if you got here without having picked your boring task of the week, go back to the previous post: the series only works in order. Pick the task first, turn it into a process second.
