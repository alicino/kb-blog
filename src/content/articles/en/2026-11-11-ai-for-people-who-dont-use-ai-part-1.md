Most people who say "I don't use AI" already have. They've asked their email client to suggest a reply, let their phone finish a sentence, accepted an automatic meeting summary.

The problem isn't starting from zero. It's not having noticed that you already started.

This is the first of five posts on the subject. They were written for people who use a little, people who don't use it, and people who think it isn't for someone like them. None of the five requires knowing how to code, paying for a subscription, or becoming an expert.

## Why "learning the perfect prompt" is a dead end

The prompt is the easy part and the part that matters least. What decides the result is whether you know how to **describe the problem**.

Someone who can't explain a task to a colleague can't explain it to AI either. The tool doesn't make up for a lack of clarity. It exposes it.

The analogy that works: you don't need to know how to drive a truck to book a move. You need to say **what you want, where, and when**.

## The anatomy of a request that works

There are four parts, in this order.

**Context.** Who you are, who it's for, what the situation is. "I'm a software salesperson selling to city governments."

**What you want.** The verb matters: summarize, rewrite, compare, list, critique, translate.

**Format.** "In 5 bullets," "in one paragraph," "in a table with deadline and owner."

**Example.** This is the part almost everyone skips and the one that improves the result most. Show a text you consider good.

The difference becomes clear in practice:

```text
Bad:
Write an email to the client.

Good:
I'm a project manager. I need to tell a client the delivery
will be 5 days late because of the hosting vendor.
Tone: professional, direct, no drama. Maximum 120 words.
End by proposing a call for Thursday.
```

The second request isn't more advanced. It's just clearer, and that clarity is what changes the result.

## The 60-second test: is this task worth it?

Three questions. If all three are "yes," it's worth trying.

1. Is it **repetitive**? You do it weekly, or daily.
2. Is it **textual**? It involves writing, summarizing, organizing, translating, comparing.
3. Is **getting it wrong the first time cheap**? You review before sending.

If the answer is "no" to any of them, it probably isn't where to start.

## Three tasks to try today

To show this isn't generic talk, I'll use three people who will show up in every post in this series.

**Camila, 24, administrative assistant.** She turns messy meeting notes into minutes with decisions and owners. Today that takes 40 minutes by hand. With AI helping, it takes 5, and she still reviews it.

**Rafael, 41, project manager.** He turns a status spreadsheet into a one-page executive summary for the board. The tedious work isn't in analyzing. It's in writing the text.

**Sandra, 35, salesperson.** She prepares for a call like this: "Give me the 5 most likely objections from a finance director hearing this price, and a short response to each." She doesn't use the text literally. She uses it so she isn't caught off guard.

None of the three tasks is technical. It's the work they already do.

## Beginner mistake number 1

Asking for something generic ("tell me about project management"), getting something generic, and concluding the tool is useless.

The problem wasn't the tool. It was the request. **Generic in, generic out.** AI doesn't guess your context. It only knows what you told it.

## Beginner mistake number 2, which is more dangerous

Trusting the output.

AI writes wrong with the same confidence it writes right. It doesn't hesitate, doesn't say "I'm not sure," doesn't add an asterisk. A made-up number shows up mid-paragraph as smoothly as a correct one.

**The rule holds for everything that follows:** you're still responsible for what goes out with your name on it. AI does the draft. The signature is yours.

## Where to start

Don't start with the app. Start with the task.

Pick **one** of the three above, do it today, and see whether the time given back justifies continuing. If it doesn't, you lost 15 minutes. If it does, you have a process, and that's what the next post in the series is about.
