There is a question almost nobody asks out loud in a meeting: **if I paste this here, where does it go?**

This is the final post in the [series](/en/articles/2026-11-11-ai-for-people-who-dont-use-ai-part-1/). The previous four covered recognising a task worth doing, turning it into a process, understanding where your real risk sits, and checking the output. What was left is the quiet worry that most people carry and rarely voice.

This is not about encryption or advanced security. It is about what not to put in the text box, and how not to depend on a single tool.

## Where the text you paste actually goes

When you paste a passage into an AI chat, that text leaves your computer and travels to another company's server. This is not theory. It is written in the terms of use, and the providers themselves publish what happens to the data.

The largest AI companies today say similar things, and the vocabulary is worth knowing because it repeats:

1. **They do not train on your data by default**, in the business offerings and on the API. OpenAI states this explicitly for its enterprise products and for the API platform.
2. **Retention is limited.** On the API, inputs and outputs are retained for up to 30 days for operation and abuse detection, then removed from their systems.
3. **Zero retention on request.** A zero data retention option exists for eligible use cases and is available on request.
4. **Encryption in transit and at rest**, with an independent security audit.

All of that is reassuring. None of it answers the question that matters. Those items describe **what the company does with the data**. They say nothing about **whether that data was allowed to leave your company in the first place**.

These are two different questions, and that is why the second one gets ignored. The first has an answer on the vendor's website. The second depends on you.

### What happened when this went wrong

In 2023, Samsung discovered that employees had pasted **internal source code** into a public AI chat. The response was severe. The company banned generative AI tools on its internal networks and on company devices, and told staff that a violation could lead to **dismissal for cause**.

The episode became famous because of its scale, and it is useful because it shows the mechanism. There was no attack, no intrusion, no security failure. There were people doing something that felt reasonable, with the tool open on screen, out of habit.

One detail in that case deserves attention. What Samsung stood to lose was not only the leak. It was **the ability to delete it afterwards**. Once data has gone to another company's server, it is no longer under your control, and the company can no longer guarantee that it is gone.

## The list of what never to paste

1. A customer's name, ID number, phone or address
2. A colleague's data: performance review, salary, personal or health situation
3. A contract, a priced proposal, a negotiated commercial term
4. Credentials: password, access key, API token
5. Proprietary company code or documents
6. Anything marked confidential, or that you would not show to an outsider

**The rule of thumb:** if you would not paste it into a group chat with people from work, do not paste it into an AI chat.

That rule works because it does not require you to understand security. It uses a test everyone already applies instinctively.

The list covers **personal data**, and in Brazil and Europe that phrase has a legal definition. Personal data is any information linked to an identified or identifiable person, and it carries its own legal protection. Pasting a colleague's performance review into an AI chat is not merely a matter of good judgement. It is processing personal data through a channel the company never authorised.

## What to do when your company has no policy

Most companies have no policy on this. That is not permission. It is the absence of a rule, and the two look identical until something goes wrong.

**Ask before, not after.** A question to your manager or the security team costs five minutes. Finding out later that you exposed customer data costs far more, and not only for the company.

**When in doubt, anonymise.** Replace the customer's name with "Client A", the amount with "amount X", the address with "address Y". For most tasks, whether you are writing, summarising, organising or rewriting, the real data makes no difference to the result. You get the same help without handing anything over.

**If your company has an approved tool, use it.** The difference between the approved tool and your personal account is exactly what happens to the data afterwards. In the company tool there is a contract, defined retention and someone accountable for the processing. On your personal account, you are the one who answers for it.

## Approved AI versus personal AI

**Company tool:** the data stays under its policy, with a contract, defined retention and clear responsibility for what may or may not happen.

**Your personal account:** the data leaves under your personal terms of service, in your name, and the company has no control or visibility over it.

Mixing the two is the most common mistake and the easiest to avoid. The rule: **work matter, work tool. Personal matter, personal tool.** Never cross them.

The reason the rule is so absolute is that the crossing is rarely a decision. It happens out of convenience. The personal chat is already open, the company login is in another browser, and the task is small. That is how the boundary disappears, one shortcut at a time.

## Do not be captive to a single tool

This is the part almost nobody considers, and it applies to any tool, not only AI.

**Keep what is yours in a format you can take away.** Plain text, a file that opens in any program. If the tool changes its price, shuts down or gets worse tomorrow, your work stays with you.

**Do not build a process that only works in one tool.** If the way you write up meetings depends on one specific app, it is tied to that app. The question that reveals the problem is simple: if this tool disappeared tomorrow, could you do the same thing somewhere else? If the answer is no, you have a dependency, not a choice.

**Test it once a year:** export everything and open it in an ordinary program. If you cannot, you do not own your material.

That idea is reasonable enough that it became law in some places. European data protection rules guarantee the **right to data portability**. You can request your data in a structured, commonly used, machine-readable format, and take it to another service. The same logic applies to any tool that stores your work, even when the law does not apply to you directly.

## How to stay current without losing your mind

The subject changes every week and you cannot follow all of it. Do not try.

**Two good sources, not twenty.** One official, which is the documentation for the tool you actually use, and one you trust for context. Two sources chosen well cover more than twenty skimmed ones.

**Ignore launches.** The new tool that promises everything rarely changes your day. What changes your day is mastering one thing. The release that matters will reach you anyway, through the two sources you chose.

**Learn when it hurts.** When a task stalls because you lack some knowledge, that is when you study it. Before that, it is content consumption dressed up as study.

## What the whole series was saying

Five posts, one idea: **AI does not replace your judgement, it amplifies what you already have.**

If you are clear about your work, it gives you time back. If you are not, it produces volume, and volume without direction is just more work to review. That is the real risk, and it does not come from asking a question badly. It comes from using the tool to look productive instead of being productive.

None of the five requires knowing how to code, paying for a subscription or becoming a specialist. Each requires one decision: use the tool to do your existing work better, not to look productive while the real work waits.
